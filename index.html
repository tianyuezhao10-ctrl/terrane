A newly encountered world moves from description, to territorial claim, to institutionalized rights.

1493

DESCRIBED / VALUE

LETTER OF COLUMBUS

Letter to Luis de Santángel

LETTER TO LUIS DE SANTÁNGEL / 1493

In his letter, Columbus described the islands he had encountered to readers in Europe, recording their lands, inhabitants, and resources. Places previously unknown to Europeans entered written accounts that could be circulated and assessed.

1494

DIVIDED / CLAIM

TREATY OF TORDESILLAS

TREATY OF TORDESILLAS / 1494

Spain and Portugal established a line of demarcation to divide their claims to overseas territories not yet fully charted by Europeans. Maps became more than instruments for describing the world; they also served to assert territorial claims.

1502

INSTITUTIONALIZED / RIGHT

BOOK OF PRIVILEGES

Libro de los Privilegios

BOOK OF PRIVILEGES / RELATED VIDEO

The Book of Privileges brought together royal grants, privileges, and legal instruments relating to Columbus's voyages. What had been presented as a "discovery" was recorded, affirmed, and translated into enduring claims of authority and entitlement.

Who held the right to travel, profit, and govern there?

Westward expansion was not only the movement of people. It was also the reorganization of land, ownership, infrastructure, ecology, and power.

1830

REMOVED

INDIAN REMOVAL ACT

PRESIDENTIAL MESSAGE ON INDIAN REMOVAL / 1832

The Indian Removal Act authorized the United States government to promote the relocation of Indigenous peoples east of the Mississippi to lands farther west. For an expanding population, the West promised new land; for peoples already living within those land relations, migration could mean forced removal.

1862

DISTRIBUTED

HOMESTEAD ACT

HOMESTEAD ACT / 1862 · DANIEL FREEMAN RECORDS / 1863–1868

The Homestead Act opened surveyed public land to eligible applicants. Land was numbered, registered, occupied, improved, and ultimately translated into private property.

1869–1873

CONNECTED / IMAGINED

TRANSCONTINENTAL RAILROAD + AMERICAN PROGRESS

Railways, roads, and telegraph networks connected western space at unprecedented speed. “Westward” became visualized as a narrative of progress: technology entered, settlements expanded, and land was developed, while Indigenous peoples and wildlife were pushed toward the edges of the frame.

WHO DEFINES PROGRESS?

1887

REORGANIZED

DAWES ACT

INDIGENOUS PERSPECTIVES ON THE HOMESTEAD ACT

The Dawes Act further altered Indigenous land systems by dividing portions of communally held land into individual allotments. A shift toward private ownership also changed social organization, cultural relationships, and ways of living.

From a signal in orbit to classrooms, public images, television screens, and public money.

1957

RECEIVED / SIGNAL

SPUTNIK

SIGNAL STUDY / RECONSTRUCTION. ORIGINAL RECORDING NOT INCLUDED IN THE PROVIDED NASA ARCHIVE.

Most people did not see the satellite in orbit. They encountered it through radio signals, news, political discussion, and the anxiety that followed. The Space Age entered public life first as sound and information.

After Sputnik, competition entered schools. Mathematics, science, engineering, foreign languages, and student loans were increasingly treated as part of national technical capacity. The race extended beyond astronauts and rockets into the classroom.
